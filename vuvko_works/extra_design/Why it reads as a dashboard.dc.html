Why it reads as a dashboard

Our screens are legible, dense and correct. That is exactly the problem: every choice we made is the choice a data tool would make. Here is what the literature says separates a game interface from an admin panel, which of those levers we have not pulled, and what to do about it in order of payoff.

I · What the field says the difference is

The load-bearing distinction is not decoration, it is where the interface lives relative to the fiction. The vocabulary everyone uses — diegetic, non-diegetic, spatial, meta — comes from a 2009 master's thesis by Erik Fagerholt and Magnus Lorentzon at Chalmers, and it has been the standard taxonomy since.1 Diegetic means the characters can see it: it is an object in the world. Non-diegetic is the 2D layer only the player sees.2

Most games are non-diegetic and that is fine — the category is not a quality ranking. What matters is the framing underneath it: in an app the UI is the product, whereas in a game the UI is competing with the product for the player's attention, so the job is conveying state without breaking immersion.3 Our panels are written as if they were the product. They are the thing being looked at, not the thing being looked through.

One honest counterweight, and it is why I am not proposing a texture pass: practitioners argue a well-executed flat UI beats a badly-executed "it matches my art direction" UI.4 The risk of staying flat is separately named, though — a generic vector language makes a game read like existing mobile or explainer-video content.5 That sentence is our diagnosis, written by someone who has never seen our file.

“The UI should feel like it belongs in the game world, not like an afterthought.”
Justinmind, game UI principles6
Their prescription is concrete: pull textures, patterns and palettes out of the game's own environments into the interface. Ours came out of a token file.

IV · Motion is the cheapest thing we are not doing

Steve Swink's model splits feel into three parts that must land in order — real-time control, then a simulated space that behaves predictably, then polish — and the ordering is the point: polish cannot rescue an interface that does not answer.8 Ours answers instantly. We are simply skipping layer three entirely.

The named techniques are small and procedural. Hit-stop — freezing for 40–80ms on a heavy impact — sells weight better than any animation.8 Easing rather than linear motion is described as the difference between movement that feels alive and movement that looks like a slideshow.8 Sound is called the most underused channel precisely because it is easy to skip.8 Practitioner write-ups add the UI-specific version: buttons that squish on press, health bars that animate damage instead of snapping.9

The same sources warn against the failure mode, and it applies double to a turn-based game read at 720p: shake and flash on everything stops meaning anything, and constant vibration makes people ill.8 The budget below is deliberately small.

V · Our type is a system problem, not a font problem

The instinct is to swap in a sci-fi typeface. The stronger argument says most game UI type failures are architectural rather than aesthetic — designers pick a face that feels right for the genre, set sizes by eye, and the hierarchy collapses on the smallest target screen.10 The recommendation is to define roles, constraints and scale ratios before choosing the typeface, with role-locked tokens per UI layer so hierarchy cannot cross-contaminate.10

There is a hard floor, too. The metric that matters is angular size, with a practical floor around 0.3° of visual angle — roughly 20–22px at 1080p console distance, 14–16px at handheld distance.10 Our 10px and 11px labels sit under that even before anyone sits back from the monitor.

And the technique that keeps a world-flavoured interface legible: hold one underlying type scale, then apply distress, grading and perspective as a layer above the type rather than baking character into the font choice — the system governs legibility, the treatment governs immersion.10 That is the permission slip for everything in section III.
